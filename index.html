Architecting Secure Multi-Agent Systems: A Governance and Scalability Blueprint

By Nelma Perera · October 2026

On September 25, 2026, I evaluated Google Cloud’s hands-on Build with Gemini workshop in Sunnyvale, California (Track 2, Platform Builders). Operating in a three-hour sandbox on a live Google Cloud project, I assumed the role of Head of AI Architecture & Security for NovaSmart—a fictional enterprise retailer facing critical multi-agent governance vulnerabilities. I systematically remediated these architecture flaws, enforcing zero-trust boundaries and robust access controls. Ultimately, the security policies held firmly under adversarial testing, while legitimate production workloads proceeded seamlessly.

Enterprise governance and zero-trust security are the core architectural challenges I navigate in our Gemini Enterprise deployments, as development teams scale agents from prototype to production. This architectural report synthesizes the fundamental principles and scalable security patterns that directly apply to our enterprise infrastructure.

    1 · The scenario

    A retailer, four AI agents, and one customer database

    NovaSmart sells in stores and online and has started using AI agents: software that reads a request in plain language, decides what to do, and calls tools, such as a database query, to do it. Unlike a chatbot, an agent holds credentials and can change things. The agents work, but nobody has a clear picture of how they are governed: who runs them, which identity they act under, what they can reach, and what happens when a customer tries to trick one.

    Two ideas from the lab carry the whole story. Front desk versus back office: some agents talk to customers; others hold confidential data and should only ever be called by other agents. Identity versus access: giving each agent its own login tells you who did what; deciding what each login may reach tells you whether it should have.

    4 · Assessment and recommendation

    Was it useful, and where does it apply?

    What made the workshop valuable

    • Real infrastructure, not slides. Identities, access lists, data permissions, and a gateway were actually changed, quirks included: one access policy could not be changed with the gcloud command-line tool, only by calling the API directly with a version tag.
    • It showed the failure modes. Switching screening on for the whole project looked like the obvious fix, but it also reads each agent’s own instructions and the data its tools return, which look just like an attack. It stopped almost all real work, 5% price matches included, and the lab notes that loosening it let the attacks through. The lab made us screen at the door instead.
    • Evidence, not opinion. The release decision rested on a scorecard.

    Limits to keep in mind

    • It is a lab. The project was small and temporary, with a handful of agents. Our own systems have more owners, more legacy identities, and more data classifications.
    • Two things were not ideal. The code-security mission, M4, was not part of the lab yet; in the guide it is CodeMender, an AI code-security agent in Public Preview. Cost, one of the trade-offs the session set out to cover, got no numbers, even though the gateway and screening add components to run and pay for.
    • A control that is on is not a control that is effective. The screening covers one agent, the front desk, and by design it fails open: if the screening service cannot run, messages pass. Monitoring has to read each control’s own log; the lab warns that a refused agent can still answer in confident prose.
    • Some access remains, and the override code stays. Identities with broad project-wide roles can still call agents around their caller lists, and the lab lists pruning them as follow-up work. The lab also points out two routes no caller list closes: the store application calls the back office directly from its code, and the store portal reads customer records under an Owner-level login. Nothing in my notes removes the override code from the front desk’s instructions, so only the screening stands in its way.
    • The evaluation was small. The lab’s method continues with a larger generated set, which my notes do not cover.

    The easiest part to reuse

    The lab’s first mission works on other projects too: an inventory of every agent and agent-like service, the identity each one acts under, and who has read the most sensitive data in the last 30 days. It changes nothing and shows where a project stands.

    The read history relies on data-access audit logging, so it is worth checking first: outside BigQuery it is off unless an administrator turns it on.

    My recommendation: five controls that carry over

    They apply to any agent that holds credentials or talks to customers, and each one can be checked rather than just written down. For teams doing the same work, they may be a useful cross-check against what is already in place.

    Questions people asked at the workshop

    Also at the workshop: one of the Google presenters spoke well of TypeSafe AI’s Jev, which was not part of the lab. TypeSafe describes Jev as a model that answers typed questions about an application’s state with calibrated probabilities instead of writing text, for decisions that code acts on, such as routing and scoring. We have not evaluated it. The day closed with demos of projects of our choice; I showed AetherIaI, a prototype that turns infrastructure intent into Terraform, and it was among the ten winners.
