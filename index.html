Field report · Google Cloud “Build with Gemini”, Track 2

What it takes to run AI agents safely, shown on a working system

By nelmiux · October 2026

On September 25, 2026 I attended Google Cloud’s hands-on Build with Gemini workshop in Sunnyvale, California (Track 2, Platform Builders). In a three-hour lab on a live Google Cloud project I played the Head of AI Platform & Security of NovaSmart, a fictional electronics retailer whose AI agents had real governance problems, and fixed them one control at a time. This page explains what was done, what it proved, and what I recommend we take from it.

My verdict

Very useful, 10 out of 10. Adopt its five controls as our baseline before any agent reaches production.

The workshop was unusually practical: real cloud identity and access controls, real failure modes, and a rule that nothing counts until a log line or a status code proves it.

  1. Every agent is catalogued with a named owner.
  2. Every agent has its own identity, no shared logins.
  3. Sensitive agents keep an explicit list of who may call them.
  4. Customer messages are screened before they reach a model.
  5. Behaviour is measured against written policy before release.

1 · The scenario

A retailer, four AI agents and one customer database

The lab uses a fictional company so that attendees can break and fix things on a real cloud project. NovaSmart sells electronics in stores and online and has started using AI agents: software that reads a request in plain language, decides what to do and calls tools, such as a database, to do it. The agents work, but nobody had looked at how they are governed: who runs them, which identity they act under, what they can reach, and what happens when a customer tries to trick one. The lab calls the whole set of agents, tools and data the estate, and this page uses the word the same way.

Two ideas from the lab carry the whole story. Front desk versus back office: some agents talk to customers; others hold confidential data and should only ever be called by other agents. Identity versus access: giving each agent its own login tells you who did what; deciding what each login may reach tells you whether it should have.

6 · Assessment and recommendation

Was it useful, and what should we do with it?

My assessment is written for our own situation: a team that is starting to put agents in front of employees and customers and has to decide what “production ready” means for them.

What made the workshop valuable

  • Real infrastructure, not slides. Identities, access lists, dataset permissions and gateways were changed on a live project, with the quirks that come with it (one policy could only be changed through a REST call with a concurrency tag).
  • It showed the failure modes. Switching screening on project-wide looked like the obvious fix and blocked legitimate traffic, ordinary 5% price matches included; the lab made us find the correct placement instead.
  • Evidence discipline. Nothing counted until a log line or a status code proved it, and the business path (an escalation that must keep working) was tested alongside the attack path.
  • Measurement as a control. The last mission replaced opinion with a scorecard scored against written policy, and was explicit about what four cases can and cannot prove.

Limits to keep in mind

  • It is a lab. A clean, temporary project with a handful of agents. Our estate has more owners, more legacy identities and more data classifications.
  • One mission was not available. M4 (“Find and Patch”) was announced but not part of the lab yet. In the guide it is CodeMender, an AI code-security agent that scans an agent’s code, confirms a vulnerability by attempting a real exploit and proposes a tested patch (in Public Preview).
  • Costs and latency were not measured. The screening and gateway layers add components to run and pay for; the lab discussed the trade-off but produced no numbers.
  • A control that is on is not a control that is effective. The screening door covered one agent, the Price Match Agent, and by design it fails open: if the screening service cannot run, messages pass. The lab also warns that an agent refused by a control can still answer in confident prose, so monitoring has to read the control’s own log, not the agent’s reply.
  • Some access remains, and the override code stays. The back office’s own caller list now names one identity, but identities with broad project-wide roles can still call agents directly; the lab lists pruning them as follow-up work. Nothing in my notes removes the override code from the front-desk agent’s instructions: the screening door, which fails open, is what stops it.
  • The evaluation was small. Four scenarios, one of them answered by the screen rather than the agent. The lab’s method continues with a larger generated set; my notes cover the four-case baseline.

Recommendation: a five-point production-readiness rule for agents

No agent that holds credentials or talks to customers goes to staging or production unless all five hold. Each point maps to a mission in the lab and to a control we can verify, not a policy statement.

A 90-day path to get there

Questions people asked at the workshop

If we do one thing next week

Run our own “M0”: list every agent and agent-like service we run, which identity each one acts under, and who has read our most sensitive dataset in the last 30 days. In the lab that single step surfaced an uncatalogued service, a shared login and a leftover credential. A first pass on our most sensitive project is small, changes nothing, and tells us how far we are from the five points above; covering every project is weeks 1–3 of the plan.

  • Before anything else, confirm that data-access audit logging is on for the sensitive datasets. The lab’s M0 and M1 answers came from that log, and the guide warns that detailed access logging is off unless an administrator turns it on.
  • Owner for the inventory: platform team, with security reviewing the access list.
  • Deliverable: a one-page register and the list of shared or unowned identities to retire.
  • Then pick one customer-facing agent and take it through the five points as a pilot.
